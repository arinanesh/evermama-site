little habits that fit
the day you already have

You pick something small enough to feel almost silly — two squats while the kettle boils, a glass of water before your coffee — and you tie it to a moment that already happens in your day, which is the part that makes it stick. Small is deliberate here, because small is what survives the weeks when nobody in the house is sleeping.

More habits are on the way, so if there's something you wish was in there, Tell us.

why it works

tiny now, enormous later

Every habit in here is pointed at a version of you who is decades away — the one who swings her kid up onto her shoulders without thinking about it, or who sneezes at school pickup and doesn't think about it again. You won't be waiting decades to feel anything, though, because most of these start paying you back long before that: sleep usually shifts inside the first week or two, and energy tends to follow it.

That far-off version of you gets a line of her own, written in your words — so years from now I can lift my kid onto my shoulders without a second thought — and it greets you every time you keep the habit and rides along on every reminder, because she is the reason any of this is worth the minutes.

inside the app

a library of habits, none of them long

Four squats by the crib, ten pages of the book on your nightstand, ten minutes with your legs up the wall, or a call to the friend you keep meaning to ring back — none of them ask much of you, and each one sits in the part of the day your body is best set up for it. There's no account to make and no name to give, so you open the app and it simply works.

hold to mark it done

Press and hold the little ring on a card and it fills in about a second, after which a line slides up to tell you who you're becoming — and that, rather than any confetti, is the whole celebration.

no streak to lose

The only number the app keeps is days kept, and missing a Tuesday doesn't reset it, so there are no badges to chase, no red marks on a calendar, and nothing lighting up at 11pm to tell you you've broken something.

reminders that pull their weight

A reminder arrives carrying your habit's name and your future-me line on the lock screen, and you can mark the habit done or push it back half an hour from there without ever opening the app.

notes to self

Every habit keeps a wall of paper notes, each with the usual snag on the front and the fix on the back — “I just forget” on one side, “a full glass in every room” on the other — and you can pin up your own.

when one starts slipping

If a habit goes three days untouched you'll get exactly one message, and it asks what's making this hard rather than telling you off; answer it or ignore it entirely and it stays quiet for two weeks either way.

nothing is final

If you tap something by accident you can undo it with a single ↺, and a redo waits there in case you change your mind again; on a day with nothing scheduled the screen simply reads a soft day and leaves you be.

the receipts

none of this is vibes

Tiny genuinely does work, and twenty years of research at Stanford's Behavior Design Lab keeps arriving at the same conclusion: the habit that survives is the one small enough to manage on your worst night. Tying that habit to something you already do has a clunky name, implementation intentions, but it's one of the most replicated findings in behavior science, and being able to picture a specific future version of yourself matters nearly as much, since Hal Hershfield's lab at UCLA found that people who can actually see her make measurably different choices today.

The timing comes out of the circadian work at Satchin Panda's lab at the Salk Institute: cortisol peaks in the morning, your muscles are strongest in the late afternoon, and melatonin climbs once the light goes, so each habit is placed where your body is already most ready for it. The library follows postpartum activity guidance from ACOG and the WHO, and it never offers you medical advice — only something short to do today that keeps paying out for decades.
